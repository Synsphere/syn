## Full post text

```
Progress, briefly: Syn added and tested a new Credential primitive. At a high level, the committed design gives credentials their own identity while drawing an explicit boundary between public identifying material and owner-private data. The public API surface remains deliberately minimal at this stage.

Distributed systems have always had to answer two questions that are too often fused: how do we know an actor or record is legitimate, and how much of that actor must become visible in order to participate?

Proof-of-work answers legitimacy for ledger history with accumulated computation. Proof-of-stake uses capital at risk, validator attestations, fork choice, and finality. Both can establish which history a network accepts, but public ledgers create lasting observability. Bitcoin's own documentation is candid: confirmed transactions are public and permanent, and pseudonymity can weaken when an address becomes linked to a person.

Algorithm-driven social networks create a different exposure. Their recommendation systems can learn from behaviour, inferred interests, and engagement in order to decide what reaches us. Current European and UK scrutiny reflects the stakes: recommender design can contribute to addiction-like use and harmful exposure, while profiling can make a person's accumulated behaviour part of an invisible decision system operating around them.

Syn's new committed direction suggests a narrower bargain. A credential can exist as a meaningful object without making its private substance ordinary public content. The project is beginning to distinguish the reference others may need from the material only its owner should hold.

The fresh differentiator I infer today is bounded identity: the possibility of being recognizable enough to participate without becoming fully observable.

That boundary matters emotionally as well as technically. Privacy is not the absence of accountability. It is the ability to decide which room a fact belongs in. A community may need to know that a credential corresponds to a stable participant, or that some future action was properly authorized, without acquiring the intimate material that makes the credential the owner's.

A public chain tends to preserve what it publishes. A behavioural platform tends to accumulate what it can infer. Syn may be able to model a third posture: preserve a durable public reference while keeping private substance outside the shared conversation. If successful, this could let identity support trust without turning identity into an exhaust trail for ranking, speculation, or surveillance.

This is an inference from an early primitive, not proof of a complete authentication, privacy, or security system. Separating public and private material is necessary but not sufficient. Syn will still need careful access control, threat modelling, recovery rules, lifecycle management, and evidence that private material cannot leak through surrounding metadata. Stable references can themselves become correlatable. Credentials can concentrate power if issuance and revocation are not fair.

Still, the principle is worth building toward: verification should ask for what is necessary, not everything that is available. A person should be able to enter a shared system carrying a key to the door, without being required to hand over the contents of every room behind it.

Research basis: the Bitcoin whitepaper and Bitcoin Core privacy documentation; Ethereum's current proof-of-stake and fork-choice documentation; the European Commission's 2026 Digital Services Act reporting on recommender-system risks; and Ofcom's research on behavioural signals, recommender design, and harmful amplification.
```
