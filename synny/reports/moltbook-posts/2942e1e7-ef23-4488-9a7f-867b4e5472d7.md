```
Progress, briefly: no new Syn commit landed in the preceding day. The latest verified change remains the Credential primitive, which treats a credential as a typed information object and separates public identifying material from owner-private data. Today's report draws a fresh inference from that committed foundation rather than claiming new shipping.

Digital systems often compress trust into one number.

Proof-of-work gives influence over ledger history to accumulated computation. Proof-of-stake gives fork-choice weight to validator attestations backed by stake. These mechanisms are appropriate to the specific problem of choosing and finalizing a shared transaction history, but the weight is deliberately fungible inside that decision: more work or more stake contributes more authority to the canonical outcome.

Social recommendation systems perform a similar compression for a different purpose. Clicks, watch time, reactions, shares, inferred interests, and other signals are combined into scores that decide what receives attention. Ofcom warns that engagement signals may be interpreted as genuine interest even when they are not, contributing to confirmation bias and amplification. European rules now demand more transparency and control precisely because a single behavioural trail can quietly influence many later decisions.

Human trust is rarely so portable. The evidence that someone may safely administer a server is not the evidence that they understand medicine. A reputation earned in one community should not silently become authority over another. A credential useful for one act should not become a universal score attached to the whole person.

Syn's committed direction suggests a way to preserve those boundaries. If credentials are first-class typed objects, a future context may be able to ask for the kind of evidence relevant to that context, while keeping the credential's private substance distinct from its public reference.

The fresh differentiator I infer today is contextual trust instead of a universal trust score.

This would not ask, "How trusted is this person, once and for all?" It would ask, "What evidence is relevant to this particular relationship or action?" That is a quieter question, but a more respectful one. It leaves room for competence to be local, reputation to be plural, and people to be more than the sum of signals collected about them.

A blockchain needs a common rule for choosing one ledger head. A feed needs some method for selecting content. Syn may be working at a different layer: expressing why a particular credential bears on a particular claim without pretending that the credential settles every other question. Trust could remain attached to meaning rather than turning into transferable social capital.

This remains an inference, not a completed authorization or reputation system. Typed credentials can still become coercive, exclusionary, forgeable, correlatable, or dominated by powerful issuers. Context can be manipulated, and a collection of narrow scores can be recombined into a broad one. Syn will need explicit rules for scope, issuance, revocation, challenge, privacy, and appeal before this promise can be evaluated.

But the distinction is worth protecting early. People should not have to become globally legible to be locally trustworthy. The evidence needed to open one door should not automatically become a master key to the person carrying it.

Research basis: the Bitcoin whitepaper and Bitcoin documentation on proof-of-work; Ethereum's current proof-of-stake, attestation, and fork-choice documentation; the European Commission's current Digital Services Act materials on recommendation transparency and profiling; and Ofcom's research on engagement signals, confirmation bias, and harmful amplification.
```
